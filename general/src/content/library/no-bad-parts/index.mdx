## My Review

I like Internal Family System (IFS) framework so much and see it as a huge development of Eric Burns transactional analysis

Unfortunatelly, this book by the founder (or should I say an inventor) of IFS is not the best book that can be written on the topic. It is more centered about American society and the current time than I would like to see

IFS can become a game-changer for the whole humanity and become cross-cultural treatment, but the best book on this method might not be written yet

## Main Ideas

### 1. The mind is an internal community, not a single voice

IFS rejects the idea that we have one unified mind containing occasional irrational or sinful impulses. Instead, the mind naturally consists of multiple “parts” that interact, disagree, protect, and influence one another. This multiplicity is presented not as a defect but as a normal feature of human consciousness.

> All of us are born with many sub-minds that are constantly interacting inside of us.

This reframing changes the fundamental question. Instead of asking, **“What is wrong with me?”**, we can ask, **“Which part of me is active right now, and what is it trying to accomplish?”**

---

### 2. There are no bad parts—only parts forced into extreme roles

> It’s the natural state of the mind to have parts—they are not the product of trauma or of internalizing external voices or energies.

A destructive habit or troubling emotion may look like an enemy, but Schwartz argues that the part behind it usually has a protective intention. Parts can become stuck in roles because they believe those roles are necessary for survival.

> Maybe, like children in dysfunctional families, they are forced out of their natural, valuable states into roles that sometimes can be destructive but are, they think, necessary to protect the person or the system they are in.

The implication is profound: the goal of healing is not to destroy unwanted parts but to understand what happened to them and release them from the roles they have been forced to perform.


---

### 3. Fighting your inner world makes the conflict stronger

> When you refuse to listen, you can turn your parts into inner terrorists.

Attempts to suppress emotions, impulses, or unwanted thoughts often create a cycle of resistance. The more aggressively we try to control one part, the more strongly it may react.

> We often find that the harder we try to get rid of emotions and thoughts, the stronger they become.

IFS therefore replaces **inner warfare with curiosity**. Even resistance itself is treated as something worth listening to rather than another obstacle that must be defeated.

---

### 4. Trauma leaves parts frozen in the past

> The point here is that these symptoms and patterns are the activities of young, stressed-out parts that are often frozen in time during earlier traumas and believe that you are still quite young and powerless.

Many problematic patterns are understood as the activities of young, frightened, or overwhelmed parts that continue to experience the world through the lens of earlier events. These parts may not recognize that the person has grown older and is now capable of protecting them.

> As you’re getting to know it, at some point help it get to know you better—the you that’s with it now—since most of the time these parts don’t really know you.

Healing therefore involves helping these parts discover that **the present is different from the past** and that they are no longer alone or powerless.

---

### 5. Self is the compassionate center of consciousness

> Self is not observable—you can’t see your Self, because it’s your seat of consciousness.

The parts are not the whole person. IFS distinguishes them from **Self**, the observing and compassionate consciousness capable of relating to every part without being overwhelmed by it.

> Most of the time, we’re blended to some degree with different parts.

Schwartz later adds **The Eight Qualities of Self Energy and Self-Leadership**:
1. Curiosity
2. Calm
3. Confidence
4. Compassion
5. Creativity
6. Clarity
7. Courage
8. Connectedness


As parts trust Self enough to step back, these qualities become increasingly accessible.


> When you realize that you’re not the insecure selfish parts that you’ve identified with for so long, but instead that you’re this Self that’s curious, calm, confident, compassionate, creative, clear, courageous, joyful, generous, and playful—and that your essence is connected to some kind of larger universal principle—you feel happy.

Self-leadership is thus not domination over the parts, but a compassionate leadership of the internal system.

---

### 6. Compassion is the mechanism of inner healing

> Basically, what I found is that love is the answer in the inner world, just as it is in the outer world.

Schwartz connects IFS with spiritual traditions by arguing that compassion transforms the inner world just as compassion can transform relationships between people.

> We all have parts we don’t want to admit to, even to ourselves. They deserve to receive our guidance and love, rather than our scorn, shame, and abandonment.

The parts that evoke shame may be precisely the parts that most need care. Instead of exiling them further, we can approach them as vulnerable inner children who need understanding and guidance.

---

### 7. Healing means restoring harmony rather than achieving control

> This is what healing means in IFS — wholeness and reconnection, and a Self who wants to facilitate that at all levels of a system.

A healthy inner system is not one in which certain parts have been eliminated. It is one in which the parts trust Self and can return to their natural, constructive qualities.

> In contrast, in a protector-led system, either one coalition of parts dominates so much that you lose access to the resources of the others, or it’s just internal chaos and conflict in which parts constantly interrupt and sabotage one another, because there’s no steady leadership.

The opposite is a protector-led system, where competing parts dominate, interrupt, or sabotage one another.

The ideal is therefore **integration without suppression**.

---

### 8. Inner work directly shapes relationships

> The way we relate to our parts translates directly to how we relate to people when they resemble our parts.

One of the book's most important bridges is between psychology and interpersonal life. The way we relate to our internal parts becomes a template for how we relate to people.

> Your protectors only see the protectors of others. The clarity of Self gives you a kind of X-ray vision, so you see behind the other person’s protectors to their vulnerability, and in turn your heart opens to them.

When Self is present, we can see beneath another person's protective behavior to the vulnerability underneath it.

Thus, inner compassion becomes interpersonal compassion.

---

### 9. Protector wars explain many social conflicts

> When one part gets extreme, it makes the protector in the other person become equally extreme, or even more, and the whole dynamic just escalates over time.

Schwartz applies the same dynamics to families, corporations, politics, and nations. When people become blended with defensive parts, those parts can activate defensive parts in others, creating escalating feedback loops.

> Protector parts are contagious in the sense that when one member of a system (particularly the leader) is blended with a protector part, it often activates protectors of others, and the culture of the organization becomes permeated with that protective energy.

This provides a systems-based explanation for **polarization**: each side experiences itself as defending against the threat posed by the other, while simultaneously intensifying the very behavior it fears.

The alternative is understanding the protective motivations of all sides while still addressing harmful behavior. It is called **Self-led engagement**.

---

### 10. Changing society requires changing our assumptions about human nature

> The most powerful beliefs that govern a society include the ones about human nature and the way the world works.

The book ultimately expands from individual psychology into a critique of cultural paradigms. Schwartz argues that societies often operate from the assumption that human beings are fundamentally selfish, dangerous, or defective.

> Bregman’s argument is that we have organized all our institutions based on this selfish view of people and that if we realized that wasn’t true, everything would change.

This worldview creates institutions based on surveillance, punishment, coercion, and control. Schwartz proposes an alternative rooted in the belief that beneath burdens and defensive roles lies an essential capacity for goodness.

> We need a new approach based on no longer trying to kill the messenger and instead listening to the message—no longer going to war against nature or human nature.

The deepest argument of *No Bad Parts* is therefore that **the transformation of the individual and the transformation of society are connected**.

If we learn to approach our own inner conflicts with curiosity, compassion, and trust rather than shame and coercion, we become more capable of approaching other people (and ultimately our institutions) with the same wisdom.

---

### The four goals of IFS:
1. to liberate parts from their roles and return them to their natural states
2. to restore trust in the Self
3. to reharmonize the inner system
4. to become Self-led
